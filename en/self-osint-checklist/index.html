A Practical Self-OSINT Checklist: What Can the Open Web Reveal About You?

A safe way to answer a useful question: what can someone learn about your public digital footprint without logging into anything?

Self-OSINT means using open sources to audit your own public exposure. It is useful for privacy reviews, account cleanup, security hygiene and anti-scam work.

1. Start with identifiers you control

  • Current and old usernames
  • Your public email addresses
  • Your own phone number visibility settings
  • Domains you own
  • Photos and documents you published yourself

2. Check username reuse

Reusing the same handle across communities can make otherwise separate public profiles easy to connect. A match is only a lead — never proof that two profiles belong to the same person.

3. Audit email exposure

Look for old public profiles, forum posts and forgotten registrations. If an address appeared in a known breach, rotate reused passwords, enable MFA and review recovery methods.

4. Inspect your photos before and after publishing

Check metadata, backgrounds, screens, documents and location clues. Many platforms strip some metadata, but you should not rely on that behavior.

5. Check domains and infrastructure you own

RDAP can show registration events and statuses. DNS records reveal where web and mail services point. IP information can identify the network and ASN, but public IP geolocation is approximate rather than a home-address locator.

Free toolkit: use the domain, DNS and IP checks on the Self‑OSINT Toolkit.

6. Separate facts from assumptions

The easiest OSINT mistake is turning a plausible match into a confident claim. Record URLs and dates, distinguish confirmed facts from hypotheses, and expect false positives.

7. Reduce what you no longer need public

  • Delete abandoned accounts.
  • Remove unnecessary contact information.
  • Use different identifiers for public and private contexts.
  • Enable MFA.
  • Review privacy settings periodically.

A simple rule

If a technique would feel invasive when used on a stranger, keep it focused on your own data or on systems you have permission to investigate.
